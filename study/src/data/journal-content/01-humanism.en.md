I've been working on my personal website for a while now.

I've made so many versions that I have started to feel discouraged and a little defeated. Classes have been intense lately, and I feel wound tight all the time. Sometimes I just want to have a good cry.

Whether I am writing frontend code, adjusting a layout, or making an animation, when something breaks the AI can read the code, find a cause, and implement a fix. So many things that once took patient trial and error can now be handled by describing a goal and getting back a fairly complete solution.

And yet, the stronger the tools become, the less certain I feel that I am doing something creative, valuable, or meaningful. If AI is doing the work, what am I doing?

Sometimes I do not even know how to revise a prompt. When I see an interaction I love, I look for open-source projects with something similar, study how they work, and ask an agent to recreate them. If the result is off, I keep adjusting it. Looking back, the process can feel like identifying, selecting, and recombining things other people have already made—perhaps without much originality or spark, perhaps even reproducing or copying them outright. When Jiahao talks about “taste,” is it a design judgment that is truly mine, or simply an increasingly fluent ability to retrieve and call up familiar aesthetic examples?

I did not used to doubt this so much. Design has always involved reference, borrowing, and transformation; every form has a history and a scheme. But when a machine can accelerate that borrowing almost without limit, some abilities I once took pride in suddenly seem less rare, perhaps even worthless. I used to think an interface looked beautiful because it took visual training, aesthetic judgment, and craft. Now an interface that looks quite good may need only a prompt and a few generations.

What unsettles me is that this embodied feeling does not stop at my design practice. I feel a little ashamed of it, but I do not know how to avoid it.

Since the semester began, the workload has been heavy: new papers, lectures, and assignments every week. During my internship I had more time to read public-account posts and follow AI news, with a strong sense of FOMO and a fear of missing a new model or workflow. Now coursework takes most of my energy. I also rely on AI more and more when I read: translate the English paper because it is too long; summarize the theory because it is too difficult; compare two articles because I want to know how they relate.

Sometimes I have supposedly finished a reading, yet the next day I can barely restate its argument. The words slide smoothly across the grooves of my brain, leaving no understanding behind. A teacher’s explanation can feel brilliant; I am even shaken by the sudden rush of knowledge. But after class I cannot think of many constructive questions. I end up reorganizing my notes and trying to carry the knowledge back into my own mind by retelling it.

I cannot blame AI for this state of mind. Fatigue, distraction, academic pressure, existential unease, and political depression all affect how a person reads and thinks. AI simply offers an unusually smooth path: when I no longer want to spend time understanding something, I can still get an answer that looks complete. Suddenly everything feels pointless. I cannot even find a gentle place to hide, so perhaps I should just give up.

Am I learning, or am I continuously consuming knowledge?

That question has gradually connected to several design-research papers and social stories I have been reading, and to a word that can sound dated in this era: humanism.

I used to feel little affinity with “user-centered” or “human-centered” approaches in design. “User needs,” especially in consumer-product discourse, can be translated into purchase intent, time spent, and conversion. “Human-centered” can also carry an uncomfortable anthropocentrism, as if everything in the world had to justify its existence through human needs.

I have had to reconsider the question—if it is a question at all.

If the main story of technological progress becomes machine capability, model iteration, computing power, and automation rates, while human judgment, experience, feeling, and even life itself become secondary conditions in a system, why should design research continue to insist on being human-centered?

Or perhaps we should ask the reverse: precisely because technology has become so powerful, do we need more than ever to reconsider why people matter?

## When outcomes become easy to obtain, what remains valuable about thinking?

On November 3, Anthropic engineer Addy Osmani published an article about critical thinking in agent workflows.

He is not a technology skeptic who refuses to use AI. He has used agents for a long time and recognizes the efficiency they offer. His concern is that as models become more capable and make fewer mistakes, people may find it harder to maintain independent judgment.

At first, we inspect the code an agent writes. Later, when it is usually right, we look only at summaries, test results, and whether the task is complete. Eventually a beautifully organized output appears in front of us, and we may approve it without really understanding it.

Osmani distinguishes degrees of reliance: cognitive offloading, deferral, and cognitive surrender. His point is not that people must do everything themselves. It is whether, when machines take over tasks, people still retain the ability to understand the system, question its conclusions, and take responsibility for them.[1]

I immediately thought of the process of revising my portfolio.

An agent writes a piece of code and tells me a problem is fixed. I open the page and see that it is still wrong, so I ask it to make another change. After several rounds, the code becomes more complicated even though the issue remains. I may know that I am unhappy with it, but not understand the implementation well enough to locate the cause.

Do I still count as the designer?

Perhaps I do: I choose a direction, evaluate the result, and remain responsible for the final work. But I also have to admit that if all I can say is “not good enough,” “make it subtler,” or “still wrong,” without explaining why an implementation fails, then at least in this part of the process I have lost some capacity for independent judgment.

The real concern may be that work we once knew how to do—and could understand—gradually becomes something we can only ask a machine to handle because we no longer need to do it ourselves.

There is a biological example often used to explain the purpose of the brain: the sea squirt.

Some sea squirts can swim freely as larvae and have neural structures for sensing their environment and finding a place to attach. After finding a suitable surface, attaching, and undergoing metamorphosis, their bodies and nervous systems change substantially; as adults they usually live attached to a surface and filter-feed. The familiar line “once a sea squirt finds a rock, it eats its own brain” is an oversimplification. Adult sea squirts do have a nervous system, and metamorphosis is more complex than “eating the brain.”[2]

Still, I find the example darkly funny.

If an organism no longer needs its former mobility to find an environment, the relevant structures may change over the course of evolution and development. If we create an external intelligence powerful enough to perform more and more of our cognitive work, will people still actively maintain abilities that everyday tasks no longer require?

And what would be the reason to maintain them?

If the answer is only to remain competitive in the labor market, that feels bleak. Thinking was never only a livelihood skill. Someone may read philosophy, study mathematics, draw, or write without any direct practical purpose. They may simply want to understand something, or find a kind of pleasure in the long practice—one that only they can experience.

We invented tools to save labor. But if a tool eventually replaces the pleasure of thinking itself, what will we do with the time it saves?

## What are the twenty-five mathematicians resisting?

A few months ago, Terence Tao published a statement on his personal blog titled “A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics.” It was initially signed by twenty-five Fields Medalists.

The statement appeared as AI capabilities in mathematics were advancing rapidly, alongside disputes over research results on major problems, publication, and priority.

At first glance, the concern is hard to understand.

Mathematics seems like one of the fields best suited to clear standards. A proposition is proved or disproved; at least in principle, there are ways to test whether a proof holds up. If a machine can solve in less time problems that have challenged mathematicians for decades, is that not a major advance?

Their criticism is that when AI companies use famous mathematical problems as benchmarks for model capability, the goals of mathematical research itself may be quietly replaced.[3]

Mathematicians do not usually solve a difficult problem just to write “solved” beside it. Valuable research may produce new concepts, proof techniques, and theoretical connections. Other mathematicians need to read, verify, simplify, and extend the results, then connect them to existing knowledge. Some methods become tools that later generations can use after long periods of discussion and teaching.

A famous problem can therefore be a marker of research progress. Its meaning lies not only in whether it is conquered, but in what people come to understand along the way.

If an AI system suddenly produces many correct but extremely complicated proofs, the situation is less straightforward.

A proof can certainly be correct. Formal verification can provide strong guarantees. But who explains it? Which ideas are worth extracting? How does the method relate to earlier work? Can students gain understanding by reading it, or can they only accept a result that has been verified?

If a piece of research leaves us with only the judgment “correct,” has the mathematical community actually gained an amount of knowledge commensurate with the number of results?

This reminds me of benchmarks and evaluations I encountered during my internship.

We can give an agent tasks and record its success rate, runtime, and cost. Improvement on these measures does mean progress under the tested conditions.

But the metric itself is not the goal. A model scoring highly on a coding task does not guarantee that the system it generates will be maintainable. An agent completing a task automatically does not mean every decision it made was authorized by the user. An AI proving a difficult theorem does not automatically mean the understanding, explanation, and transfer of knowledge that mathematical research depends on have taken place.

We choose a measurable result to assess progress in an activity. Technology becomes very good at optimizing that result. Then people start to forget why the activity mattered in the first place.

Mathematical research has long relied on papers, public proofs, peer review, citation, and attribution to sustain trust in its community. Large AI systems, however, are often controlled by commercial companies; their training materials, internal reasoning, and computing resources are not always open to others.

A result may be publicly verifiable while the system that produced it still has transparency problems. In its 2026 announcement about the Navier–Stokes problem, OpenAI published a proof and some formal materials, but that did not make the internal model's full research process a completely open public resource. After questions about its relationship to other researchers' work, OpenAI later published an investigation and denied that related Codex inputs had influenced the internal model.[4]

AI capability is built on a long accumulation of human knowledge, including published research, code, and arguments. Scientific communities encourage sharing because research can continue only when others can read, question, and adapt existing work.

Now these materials also become an important foundation for training high-capability models. Models produce results faster and faster, while parts of the research process are led by a small number of companies with access to advanced systems.

The relationship of sharing has changed: knowledge can still be used, but the capabilities produced from it are not necessarily opened to the original knowledge community to the same degree.

The Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics, published in June 2026 and supported by the International Mathematical Union, emphasizes research transparency, citation, responsibility, and the status of human authorship. It is concerned with more than whether a proof is correct; it also asks how mathematics can continue as a communal practice.[5]

In an interview, Fields Medalist Deng Yu said that when researchers already have a clear direction, AI can take on some technical details. Even when AI explores new ideas, people can advance the research by understanding, selecting, and developing the results. He does not believe that growing AI capability necessarily means the end of mathematical research.[6]

That disagreement matters: one side worries that technological competition will damage the community of knowledge, while the other recognizes that technology can help researchers tackle problems once constrained by time and computing power. Neither position is simple technological optimism or pessimism.

When machines can take on a large share of knowledge production, how should we design new research institutions? How can we let machines help people move beyond their former limits without allowing people to lose opportunities to develop understanding, train researchers, and ask new questions?

This question is just as urgent for design.

Design has never been only about making a useful tool. It also helps determine how tools enter people's lives, how people and tools divide work, and which activities become easier or harder under a given technological arrangement.

## Who owns the “taste” in AI design?

At this point I circle back to my portfolio. I really do feel that “taste” may be a pseudo-problem.

There are so many design websites, open-source projects, interaction examples, and visual styles to reference. If you like a website, you can analyze its type, spacing, motion curves, and layout. If an animation feels off, you can look for another way to make something similar. Break it apart, recombine it, collage, reproduce, transfer, vary—and so on.

If innovation is only a new collage of existing forms, a great deal of design work seems reducible to searching, selecting, and combining.

This makes me think of music. I have been hoping to see The Pains of Being Pure at Heart. They may not be especially original. But music does not always move us through virtuoso technique or a language no one has heard before. Many styles have clear historical sources; you can often hear what period or kind of music they borrow from and pay tribute to.

That does not stop me from liking them. Even if they are not singular, I can still empathize with this ordinary kind of beauty.

Why must someone invent a completely unprecedented harmony or instrument to be considered creative?

This seems to reveal a contradiction: I accept repetition, borrowing, and stylistic continuity in music, but I often feel bored when AI design recombines earlier work. Perhaps the difference is not originality alone.

A creator can know what they want to express and why a particular form is necessary, even when using existing forms. Another situation begins with an effect that is already widely admired, then applies it to a problem that has not yet been thought through.

I cannot conclude that human artists always have the first kind of intention, or that AI-assisted work always falls into the second. Commercial design contains plenty of unconsidered repetition, and collaboration with AI can also produce thoughtful, distinctive work.

If taste only recognizes things that are already good, it can easily become a retrieval and ranking ability. The harder part is deciding what is worth making in a situation with no accepted answer—and why one is willing to take responsibility for that choice.

I used to feel frustrated when a technical limitation made something impossible. Now that some technical barriers have eased, I face a different question more often: even if I can make any of these things, what do I actually want to make?

Perhaps that is what makes design judgment so difficult.

## What is design research actually studying?

Last week I read Pieter Desmet's doctoral dissertation, *Designing Emotions*. I have always felt puzzled by emotion typologies.

A person's response to a product can be complicated, but researchers need to abstract it into forms that can be named, classified, and compared. Does this process lose the original nuance?

I often think about “guilty pleasure.” A pleasure may contain guilt, and the guilt does not necessarily cancel the pleasure. We can analyze the components of different emotions, but after splitting them into concepts, has the mixed feeling still been fully described?

Desmet's work is interesting because it does not treat a product's emotion as a property of the product alone.

In his appraisal model, the product stimulus, a person's concerns, their appraisal, and their emotion are related. Someone may like a product because its shape fits their preferences; someone else may react differently because the product obstructs a goal or conflicts with a personal standard.

The dissertation further discusses appraisal types such as appealingness, motive compliance, legitimacy, and novelty, and explores research tools such as PrEmo for measuring emotions elicited by products.[7]

Of course, these frameworks simplify reality. Perhaps the abstraction is not meant to achieve absolute accuracy but to create a kind of beauty in human cognition. Organizing something complex and ambiguous into an intelligible order can help designers in practice and make it possible to analyze wicked problems.

In *Twenty Moods*, Desmet later describes twenty moods across feeling, perception, reaction, tendency, liking, and disliking.

According to the papers and method notes in the booklet, the researchers analyzed 135 mood words and combined researcher introspection with mood diaries and other materials, repeatedly forming and revising the typology.[8]

When I first read it, I kept asking why there were twenty moods, why six dimensions, and what happened to feelings that could not be classified.

Later I attended a conversation between Professor Haian Xue and Pieter. Hearing that science itself needs to simplify and model reality made me realize how naïve my earlier thought had been. I was a practice-oriented design student who had never received academic writing training or guidance in design-research methods.

If a theory had to reproduce the world it studies in full, it could not become a theory. Abstraction is not itself a mistake. Often we need concepts, models, and categories because we cannot directly handle the full complexity of reality.

The questions are why researchers choose these concepts and whether the simplification has explanatory power.

Turning a difficult-to-articulate subjective experience into knowledge that others can understand, discuss, test, and use is itself a complex creative task.

That does not mean a framework can escape rigor just because it inspires designers. We still need evidence about whether a classification has explanatory value, whether a measurement tool is valid, and whether it can help designers make better products.

But we should not dismiss the value of abstraction, classification, and concept creation simply because a model cannot encompass every person's experience.

At that point I began to understand a little better why teachers were willing to spend years studying things that seemed so small.

A designer can express an understanding by making an artifact. A researcher has another possibility: to abstract a relationship in an experience that has not yet been adequately explained and make it knowledge that others can use, criticize, and develop.

That takes considerable intellectual ability.

It still requires human interest, judgment, and creativity.

## Does human-centered thinking become too anthropocentric?

Let me separate two positions.

One treats humans as the sole source of all value in the world and believes other beings matter only when they meet human needs.

The other recognizes that, in the technologies and social institutions we are building, human life, experience, dignity, judgment, and autonomy should be taken seriously and protected.

The first is an anthropocentrism that deserves scrutiny. The second can form a humanism capable of reflecting on itself.

Taking human circumstances seriously may also mean taking the relationship between people and the nonhuman world seriously. A person's health and well-being depend on the environment; their ability to act depends on technology, institutions, and other forms of life. Excluding these relationships from design does not necessarily serve people's long-term interests.

So the “human-centered” approach I want to discuss does not mean that all design should revolve around people's immediate preferences, nor that technology must remain within the limits of current human abilities.

## An exhibition about death

Over the weekend I visited an exhibition that brought death into everyday life. Perhaps because it was raining, I felt sad and could hardly stop myself from crying. What stayed with me were two phrases: the sincerity of art, and the artist's insincerity.

Some works made me feel that their makers had seriously faced the subject they were addressing. Other works made me wonder whether an artist had borrowed the heavy subject of death to give a piece an instant appearance of depth.

Our teachers often share examples of ancient Chinese tombs, ideas about death, and aesthetics. I suddenly realized that modernization has changed not only the technologies and institutions for dealing with death, but also how we understand it, remember the dead, and face the fact that our own lives will end.

Some older rituals no longer fit easily into everyday life; traditional language around death is also moving further away from us. Traditional funeral practices were not all reasonable; some came with clear hierarchies and heavy financial burdens. Changes in modern medicine, public health, and funeral systems have also reduced a great deal of suffering.

There is a difference between *dying* and *death*.

The first can refer to the process of undergoing death; the second can name the event, state, or outcome. Death is not only a recordable end to life. For the people who experience it and those around them, it also involves time, relationships, care, fear, regret, and meaning.

Pascal wrote in *Pensées* that a human being is a fragile reed in nature, but a reed that thinks.

I used to think the line emphasized human reason and self-awareness. On rereading it, I noticed that Pascal goes on to discuss the fact that people know they will die.[10] This kind of thought is not only the ability to calculate and reason; it also includes an awareness of our finite condition.

How people understand their place in the world.

If human dignity rests entirely on a ranking of capabilities, then as machines surpass people, people can only keep retreating.

A PhD in design might be interesting. After reading parts of Dewey, Buchanan, and Desmet, then listening to teachers discuss research methods, I am beginning to understand the appeal of this work.

Design research can transform judgments that depend on personal experience and are hard to pass on into knowledge others can understand, discuss, test, and develop.

Recognizing a question worth studying in the world, deciding which experiences to preserve and which relationships to abstract, and finding appropriate methods and evidence for that abstraction all require creativity.

Different researchers also have different interests. Pieter Desmet's work moved from product-emotion research toward well-being; his concerns expanded from how a particular product evokes emotion to how design can participate in people's happiness and long-term lives. Haian Xue and Desmet's 2019 paper “Researcher Introspection for Experience-Driven Design Research” further discusses how a researcher's own first-person experience can become legitimate material for design research.[11]

This reminds me of Pieter saying in a lecture that his advisor once told him not to lose his designer identity.

I used to have a stereotyped picture of academic research, as if researchers should remove themselves as much as possible until only objective evidence, standard methods, and conclusions remain. It is clear now that a researcher's personal experience is not necessarily only a source of interference to be eliminated.

Why someone is especially sensitive to a subject, why they follow a particular emotion over time, and why they want to study a question others have overlooked may all connect to their experience and identity.

Introspection needs methods and reflection. We must consider which questions it suits and where it fails to represent other people's experiences. Design research preserves an interesting possibility: researchers can begin with questions they genuinely care about and try to turn them into knowledge others can participate in.

The world needs people willing to understand a question over time, judge why it matters, and ask where these research activities should take people and society.

What we should defend is people's opportunity to participate in producing knowledge, and the possibility of forming their own judgment, experience, and meaning in the process.

A person studies mathematics not only to get the correct answer; studies design not only to make beautiful interfaces; and reads philosophy not only to memorize the theories of a few thinkers.

If AI eventually creates a way of working in which people only issue instructions and accept answers, gradually no longer needing to understand or take responsibility for the judgments involved, I think again of the sea squirt that found a place to attach.

A life seems to involve continually encountering new uncertainties and trying to live with them. If research can help someone remain curious about the world, keep spending time identifying questions and forming understanding, and even find some joy along the way, that already has considerable value.

The tired phrase “human-centered” does not mean that technology must always obey every immediate human desire, or that humans have the privilege of explaining and controlling the whole world.

Even when we can create technological systems this powerful, we can still treat human circumstances as questions worth understanding, let human judgment help shape technological purpose, and preserve people's room to learn, create, make mistakes, object, and choose again.
