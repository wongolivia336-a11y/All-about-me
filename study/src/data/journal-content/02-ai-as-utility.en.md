## Preface

Intelligence does not grow underground. Why, then, do we so naturally place it alongside coal, oil, and electricity—as if humanity's long accumulation of knowledge were a mineral waiting to be extracted? The metaphor may contain a quiet arrogance. We feel uneasy when human creations are redefined as resources, yet rarely question why we have long treated nature as a resource by default. Rivers are measured as volumes of water; forests as timber reserves; underground deposits as future production capacity. Under these apparently neutral names, rich and complex forms of existence recede, leaving value that can be measured, traded, and extracted.

This makes me think of Bruno Latour's discussion of relations between humans and nonhumans. In actor-network theory, rivers, soil, microbes, and even technological devices are not silent backdrops that human society may arrange at will. They participate in and change the network of relations in their own ways. From this perspective, we might reconsider the positions implied by the word “resource.” When something is brought into human systems of production and calculation, how much of its own agency, history, and connections with other beings is hidden by a story centered on human needs? If we believe human knowledge should not become an object of unrestricted extraction without discussion, what makes us so certain that everything in nature is simply there, waiting for us to take it?

In the age of generative AI, this association has acquired an almost absurd real-world illustration. Public court documents in 2026 described Anthropic's “Project Panama,” in which the company reportedly bought large quantities of physical books, cut off their spines, scanned their pages, and sent the paper for recycling to create a digital text library for model development. Books, once material objects to read, pass on, and keep, were converted into data that could be processed at scale. The complex relationships embodied in their paper, bindings, authors, and historical contexts risked being simplified along the way. Of course, buying, scanning, and training involve different legal and ethical questions, and treating something as a resource does not necessarily mean exploiting it. Still, this symbolically powerful process makes us ask: who gained the power to redefine what these things were for, and who gets to decide how they are redistributed after being extracted?

What may deserve our attention is not that intelligence has finally become as easy to call on as water, electricity, or coal. It is that we have long been accustomed to understanding the world through the lens of resources. Only when this logic is applied so visibly to our own knowledge and creativity do we begin to recognize that a resource is never merely something that can be used. The word also contains questions of who gets to name it, who has the right to take it, and who must bear the consequences of its transformation.

## 01 · THE SOURCE

### Where does intelligence come from?

Water can be drawn and coal can be mined. Electricity, by contrast, must be generated, converted, and distributed before it reaches a room. Calling all three basic resources tends to erase their different conditions of formation. Intelligence is even harder to imagine as a natural deposit simply waiting to be mined: it includes individual cognition, but also language, institutions, works, and knowledge accumulated across generations.

A model's ability to explain a theory, write code, or imitate a style does not mean that those capabilities existed as finished products inside its training data. The patterns it uses come from large amounts of text and other materials, as well as statistical learning, model architecture, engineering, and continuing human labor. In this process, dispersed cultural and professional practices are reorganized into a technical capability delivered through an interface. The people, historical situations, and paths of transmission that carried those practices may become less visible.

In the *Grundrisse*, Marx discussed the “general intellect”: as large-scale machine industry developed, broad social knowledge became embodied in the production system and operated as a productive force. He was writing about nineteenth-century machines and factories. To equate that passage directly with today's large models would erase enormous differences in technology and institutions. Still, it reminds us that a highly concentrated productive capability may rest on knowledge contributed by far more people than those who own the final product.[2]

This observation does not automatically amount to a moral judgment against model companies. Training systems, building computing infrastructure, improving algorithms, and providing dependable services all require new investment, professional judgment, and people who take risks. Recognizing the social origins of knowledge does not erase the legal and ethical boundaries around individual works, nor does it imply that every intelligent service should be free.

The change worth examining happens on another level. Capabilities once dispersed across books, classrooms, workshops, and professional communities are now gathered through models and interfaces, then redistributed through subscriptions, usage counts, computing quotas, and product tiers. Users can see the result easily, while the chain of knowledge supporting it is folded into a nearly invisible technical system. As calling on intelligence becomes ordinary, our understanding of how it should be accessed—and who decides the terms of access—quietly changes.

## 02 · THE FUNNEL

### Available to everyone, yet layered in ways we cannot see

In October 2026, Andrej Karpathy used a sharp funnel to describe the gap in AI use he had observed. At the widest end are many people who have barely encountered large language models. Further in are people who occasionally use free chat tools. Deeper still are people who can embed frontier models in professional tasks and continuously call on complex tools. The innermost group, with access to unreleased systems, is smaller still.[3]

He gave some strikingly unequal estimates while also making clear that they were rough calculations, not a rigorously sampled picture of the world's population. The funnel is therefore better treated as a sketch for thinking about difference than as precise global demographics. Someone may not use AI by choice, because they have no need for it, or because they do not trust it; not every absence means exclusion. Some people may be opting out.

Still, the sketch stays with us because it breaks the overly broad word “adoption” into parts. One person may once have asked a chatbot about the weather. Another may orchestrate models, codebases, retrieval systems, and automation to complete complex work. Both count as AI users, but they have very different powers to act. Similar product names and identical chat windows can conceal this difference, leading us to mistake the visibility of technology for the accessibility of capability.

This distance has to do with money, but it is about much more than money. A stable network, a suitable device, language support, education, payment methods, and the expertise needed to understand a model's limits all shape what someone can gain from intelligent tools. For users already familiar with technology, these conditions can recede into the background as an unremarkable part of life. For others, they may be successive thresholds on the way to using a capability.

The barriers AI has helped me cross are real. But my personal experience is not enough to prove that the same liberation has occurred in everyone's life. As intelligent services become involved in learning, job seeking, and work, differences in people's starting points may accumulate into new differences in opportunity.

Technology has always spread unevenly. Computers, the internet, and mobile devices were once expensive and scarce before becoming more common. The fact that stratification exists today does not prove that AI must make society less equal. What deserves long-term attention is whether, as this technology changes from an optional consumer tool into an important condition of social participation, today's temporary gaps in access will shrink or become fixed in new institutional arrangements.

## 03 · BEHIND THE VEIL

### Imagine not knowing who you will be

Imagine a question about subscription prices in a situation where you cannot yet see your own account. In a future society where everyone may need intelligent assistance, you have the chance to help set basic rules: what support public schools should receive, which basic functions should be widely available, how complex models should be priced, and whether vulnerable groups need additional protection. But before the rules are set, you do not know your own income, education, devices, or living conditions.

In this thought experiment, you might be a graduate student with several computers and fluent access to English-language materials. You might instead be a worker who needs help understanding an employment contract, or a child who can only get online through an old phone and hopes AI might offer tutoring. Any of these identities could be yours. You cannot choose the most advantageous position in advance and then decide who should bear the greater cost.

The experiment draws on the “veil of ignorance” proposed by political philosopher John Rawls. In *A Theory of Justice*, Rawls imagined people temporarily unaware of their social positions, talents, and life circumstances in order to consider which basic social principles might be chosen fairly. Rawls never designed subscription plans for AI products. Applying his method to intelligent services today is a bounded, contemporary use of the idea.[4]

From one's own position in life, a monthly membership fee may be an expense too small to consider. But when we cannot know in advance who we will be, we have to reconsider conditions that are easy to overlook. If someone has limited ability to pay, little language support, or a school without appropriate devices and teaching, could they gradually lose important opportunities to learn and work because they cannot use intelligent services effectively?

The veil of ignorance cannot determine a fair price, nor can it prove that everyone must receive exactly the same amount of computing power. But it changes the starting point for discussing distribution. When we temporarily set aside the conveniences we already have, access to intelligent services becomes a public question. Some differences can be handled through personal preference and market choice; others may affect the basic opportunities people need to participate in society. The boundary between the two is shifting as technology enters everyday life.

## 04 · CAPABILITY

### How far is owning a tool from having a capability?

Following this line of thought, the most obvious solution may seem to be lowering the price so more people can enter the system for free. Yet even if every account cost nothing, users could still face very different real-world conditions. Being able to open the same software does not guarantee enough time, skill, environment, and support to turn its functions into something a person can actually do.

The capability approach developed by economist and philosopher Amartya Sen gives us language for this difference. It asks what actions and ways of living a person can actually pursue, rather than only counting the resources they possess. The same goods or institutional rights can translate into very different freedoms once they pass through different bodily conditions, social environments, and personal circumstances.[5]

Imagine two students with the same free AI account. One has a computer, a stable network, and a teacher willing to discuss questions. They know how to verify a model's answer and can place generated code in a real project and test it repeatedly. The other can use the tool only occasionally on a phone with an unreliable signal, lacks relevant instruction, and does not know where to turn when an answer is wrong. Their service entry points look the same, but the help they can actually receive in the learning process may be very different.

This difference has a clear material context. In a 2025 report, the World Bank described four foundations for participating in AI development: connectivity, compute, local context, and skills. It noted that these conditions are unevenly distributed among economies at different income levels. Differences in networks and computing resources are not identical to differences in the ability to use AI, but they remind us that opening an interface alone cannot remove the infrastructure gap behind it.[6]

At the same time, we should be careful not to present inequality as a one-way consequence of technology. Lower-cost models, open weights, local deployment tools, and new learning resources are also making capabilities once dependent on expensive expert services available for more people to try. For someone who has never had a chance to study programming, drawing, or a foreign language systematically, a first useful encounter with AI may be precisely the beginning of crossing an existing barrier.

The fairness of intelligent services cannot be reduced to giving everyone the same technology product. It also depends on whether different people can actually use the product within their own circumstances. To understand whether technology expands or narrows gaps, we need to examine tool prices, public education, environments of use, institutional support, and the long-term accumulation of capability together. Only then can “inclusion” mean more than an airy phrase in a product launch.

## 05 · THE COMMON DOOR

### How should the door be opened?

As I write this, I return to Manchester in 1852. The books in that library still had to be purchased, and the building still needed to be maintained. Borrowers no longer had to pay the full cost of every book they took because society chose to share those costs through fundraising, public taxes, and institutional arrangements. Its significance was not that it eliminated the cost of circulating knowledge, but that it tried to change the economic threshold a person first had to cross to reach it.

Of course, a library cannot solve educational inequality on its own. Literacy, working hours, where people live, and cultural experience still shape who can read, what they understand, and who has the time to turn reading into new opportunities. That is why the library is better understood as a historical institutional experiment than as an answer we can copy mechanically today. Public access can widen some possibilities, but it cannot replace all the social conditions that make those possibilities real.

Today's intelligent services are more complex than a library. Generative models have ongoing computing costs and must contend with reliability, safety, and misuse. Frontier research also requires substantial investment. Commercial professional services, tiered access, and reasonable returns can coexist with a degree of public accessibility. How different uses should be supported requires open discussion and concrete institutional design.

If intelligence gradually becomes an important condition for learning, work, and expression, a social arrangement worth considering might include affordable basic services, access for schools and public institutions, adaptation to different languages and regions, digital-skills education, and a technical environment where people can choose local or open tools. These options still involve arguments about cost, priority, and risk. But they mean we do not have to entrust the whole question of fairness to a single market trend: prices continuing to fall.

What we need to preserve is the public imagination opened by the water-electricity-coal metaphor. Once a capability becomes a basic condition in many people's lives, how it reaches people in different circumstances deserves attention as serious as its performance and commercial value. Models will continue to improve. Beyond the story of technical progress, there is a less visible historical thread recording whether society is willing to create opportunities for more people to approach this capability.

After I complete a new prototype, I will probably still open AI out of habit, keep asking questions, revise, reject answers, and choose what seems useful from its suggestions. The freedom I experience through this technology is real. I have no reason to pretend I stand outside it, and I do not want the existence of distribution problems to erase the fact that AI has lowered barriers to learning and creation for many people.

Still, the next time I hear “intelligence is becoming a resource like water, electricity, and coal,” I may first think of that nineteenth-century library. The ideas in its books already existed. For them to enter more people's lives, someone had to raise funds, build a place, set rules, and recognize that the opportunity to read deserves consideration beyond an individual's purchasing power.

The door in front of us is, of course, more complicated. The capabilities inside it keep changing, and the people outside have different needs. Even if no single solution can eliminate every inequality, we can at least continue to ask how many people, given their actual circumstances, can truly reach the switch as intelligence becomes both more and less important.
